Mug — rewards for bringing your own cup

A reusable-cup rewards concept for local cafés. I created the planning persona, brand identity, and all illustrations, then worked with a four-person team on the app flows and screens.

TEAM4 people · class project
TIMELINE3 months (one semester) · 2022
TOOLSFigma · Whimsical · Qualtrics

My partPersona, brand identity, and all illustrations. Flows, wireframes, final screens, and animations are team work.

THE DESIGN BET:
CONVENIENCE + REWARD

The concept assumes that forgetting, time pressure, and an unclear reward can get in the way of bringing a reusable cup. It explores convenience and incentives rather than treating awareness alone as the problem.

FORGETTING, HURRY,
AND NO REASON TO CARE

Reminders address forgetting. A barcode offers a direct route to payment. Rewards provide an immediate reason to bring a cup. Their relative value is unvalidated.

“How might we make being sustainable the convenient choice, not the extra chore?” THE HMW THAT GUIDED THE DESIGN
JOURNEY

The journey map tracks the habit change and marks where an app can step in: reminders when resolve is fresh, progress tracking when it fades, rewards at the register.

REWARD BRINGING,
NOT BUYING

Mug explores rewarding the reusable-cup behavior itself: any cup can count. The original concept expanded into six features; a future pilot would narrow this to verification and reward settlement.

  • Accounts tracking every cup saved, over time and per café
  • A feed where friends see and nudge each other's savings
  • Saved payment for faster ordering and instant discounts
  • Custom avatars: a mug made yours
  • A barcode front and center
  • Nearby cafés and their available rewards

THE CORE
REUSE LOOP
  1. Find a café. The map and café detail introduce the place and offer.
  2. Bring a cup. Any reusable cup is the premise; buying a branded mug is not required.
  3. Confirm the reuse. The customer barcode is shown. Staff verification is the missing service step.
  4. See the progress. Cup history and rewards carry the result back into the app.

The community feed and customizable mugs add personality around this loop. They do not resolve the verification step.

OPEN
QUESTIONS

The customer-facing screens leave three service questions to resolve:

  • Verification. A barcode identifies an account; it does not confirm that someone brought a reusable cup. The merchant needs a way to record that step.
  • Reward funding. The screens offer a first drink and a reward after five cups. Who pays for each offer, and how does a café settle it?
  • Hi-fi validation. The historical screens were not usability-tested. A new version would need readable text contrast and a complete merchant flow before a pilot.

Looking back, I would start with verification:

I would explore whether staff could confirm a reusable cup alongside the existing checkout. That depends on the café’s software, equipment, and rush-hour routine. The next step is to map that workflow with staff before designing the merchant screen.

The pilot would need to cover a valid reuse, a duplicate attempt, an unavailable reward, and a failed connection. Payment and cup verification should be evaluated separately.

No merchant pilot was run. Its first question would be whether verification fits the checkout without making the reusable cup harder to use.

WHERE IT LANDED

Mug brings a recognizable identity to a simple habit: bring a cup, record a reuse, earn a reward. My next step would be to resolve that loop with café staff before expanding the social features.
