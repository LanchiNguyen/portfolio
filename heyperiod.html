Hey Period — brand and app for a new health test

Hey Period was developing a blood test that uses menstrual blood. Before launch, the team needed a brand, a waitlist site, and a visual system for the app. I designed all three in two months.

CLIENTHey Period · femtech startup
ROLEProduct designer · only designer
TIMELINE2 months · 2021
TOOLSFigma
Mine from the startBrand identity, marketing site, assets, and marketing copy.
Built on team workThe app began with team-supplied low-fidelity flows. My scope was the visual system and the navigation and interaction changes shown below.
StatusPre-launch handoff

Project status: the work was handed off during R&D. I did not have direct access to users. Launch outcomes are unconfirmed.

EXPLAIN THE SERVICE,
THEN THE NEXT STEP

The proposed service asked people to collect an unfamiliar sample and share personal health information. The design needed to explain the process clearly and make the next step easy to find, while keeping the tone approachable.

DESIGNING WITH
ASSUMPTIONS

The project boards and period-tracking references helped frame the design. Without direct user access, I used planning personas to explore different needs rather than treating them as research findings.

These project-era reference categories suggest three design questions; they are not a current competitive analysis:

CompetitorReference categoryVisual conventionImplication for Hey Period

The two planning personas ask different questions of the same service: how it fits an existing tracking routine, and how clearly it explains its limits to someone with a specific health concern.

HOW PLAYFUL COULD
A BLOOD TEST FEEL?

The main brand decision was how much playfulness the product could carry before the test stopped feeling serious.

Pink establishes the category.
Pink connects the identity to period care. The broader palette and geometric shapes give the brand room beyond that familiar cue.
Supporting colors are whimsical on purpose.
Periwinkle, marigold, mint, and blush do the warmth job. The supporting colors carry into packaging and app categories; warmth cannot replace clear collection instructions.
Poppins, for both jobs.
Geometric enough for UI sizes, round enough for display sizes. One family across brand, web, and app kept a two-month timeline realistic.

Looking back, these are the tradeoffs I see in the visual direction. This is a current assessment, not a record of alternatives explored with the team in 2021:

DirectionWhat it emphasizesTradeoff in this brief
Clinical teal (the diagnostics look)A clinical visual toneCould emphasize testing, but would need other ways to express the intended warmth
Millennial beige (the DTC wellness look)A quiet, familiar wellness aestheticRisk: reads as skincare and obscures the prelaunch health concept
Familiar pink + whimsy (chosen)Period-care cues with a broader supporting paletteUsed in the project; differentiation and clear explanation still need testing
ONE JOB: EXPLAIN IT
AND GET A SIGNUP

The waitlist page had to explain the test before asking for an email. I designed every asset and refined the copy with the Head of Product.

The biomarker map organizes the explanation.
The mockup groups biomarker labels by topic so the content is easier to scan. A launched service would need clinical review of every test claim and explanation; visual credibility cannot establish medical validity.
Goal-based sections instead of feature lists.
"Solving your period problems / Optimizing your fertility / Tracking your sexual health", each with a chart card previewing the data. This organizes the intended information around a person's question; the health outcomes shown are not established by this design work.
How-it-works in exactly three steps.
Choose what to track, receive the kit, learn about your health. The design makes the mail-in step explicit. Whether that explanation earns trust remains a research question.
CLARITY
& WARMTH

I used different parts of the system for different jobs:

  • Clarity: separate information groups, a visible sample process, and a dedicated Insights tab.
  • Warmth: the supporting palette, rounded shapes, and illustrated insight cards.

Before launch, I would check understanding before measuring conversion:

QuestionHow I would checkWhat would change
Can someone explain the service and its limits?Ask them to describe it after reading the page, without supplying medical or trust-related prompts.Revise explanations that create unsupported expectations.
Are the sample steps and assistant role clear?Ask what they would do next and who they think is responding in the chat.Change instructions, labels, or navigation where the next step is misunderstood.
Does the page support an informed signup?After comprehension review, establish a conversion baseline by traffic source.Investigate drop-off alongside what people understood; a signup alone does not establish trust.
CONSTRAINTS
& SELF-CRITIQUE

Reviewing the original mockups in 2026, I would change two things before putting them in front of users:

  • Contrast. Both the category chips and the insight cards use white text on pale backgrounds. Dark text needs to carry through the whole card, including its labels and body copy.

WHAT I WOULD DO NEXT

I would first check whether people can explain the service, its limits, and the sample steps in their own words. Their misunderstandings would guide the next revision.
