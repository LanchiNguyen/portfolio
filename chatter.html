Chatter — a pivot story

A solo, one-month concept from March 2020: spontaneous, time-limited video calls with friends. It's here for what happened in the middle.

OVERVIEW

Mid-pandemic, keeping friendships alive meant Zoom fatigue and calendar Tetris. Young adults needed a way to keep up with long-distance friends that didn't run on endless texting or scheduled calls.

RESEARCH

I ran 30-minute interviews with 8 people aged 18–25 about how they keep in touch, then affinity-mapped the notes. Two insights drove everything after:

Catching up is hard because context gets lost, so conversations stay superficial.
SYNTHESIS · INSIGHT 01
Calling bonds more and tires less than texting, but scheduling it is the chore.
SYNTHESIS · INSIGHT 02
PERSONA

The insights condensed into Christy: a busy student who wants to swap life updates with long-distance friends without writing long paragraphs to everyone.

“How might we redesign texting and calling to fit long-distance friendships?” THE HMW BEHIND BOTH IDEAS
IDEA 01
MICRO-JOURNALS

The first idea crossed texting with journaling: a semi-private journal shared with chosen friend groups. One update reaches everyone, nobody owes a reply. I studied Tiny Thoughts, Daylio, and Fabriq, then wireframed entries, group sharing, and a relationship score nudging you toward neglected friends.

THE PIVOT

I walked people through a Figma Mirror prototype [ADD: how many, and whether they were the original interviewees]. The feedback was polite, but nobody said they'd use it. Two comments kept repeating:

"It'd be nice to have more modes of communication, like videos or pictures."
PARTICIPANT · ROUND 1
"I still have to make efforts to update it."
PARTICIPANT · ROUND 1

The answer was already in my interview notes: people prefer calling because it's higher-fidelity and lower-effort. I was building a better way to text when the research said to fix calls. So I pivoted.

IDEA 02
LOW-PRESSURE CALLS

Glimpse had already proven strangers would do 2-minute video calls; my move was applying that cap to existing friendships, where the pickup-friction problem lives. Houseparty and Cocoon supplied the drop-in-room pattern.

  • Start a chatter: open a room with a topic and a 2-minute cap
  • Save a chatter: post the call's metadata (never a recording) to your feed
  • Join a chatter: hop into a friend's live room from the feed
  • Receive a chatter: answer now, or pass without guilt
ROUND 2
FEEDBACK

The second walkthrough [ADD: n] got a different response: people described situations where they'd use it, which round one never produced. The consistent complaint, a forced default time limit, became a user-set limit in the hi-fi pass.

"I love it!! Such a fun and quick way to catch up with friends."
PARTICIPANT · ROUND 2
"I can see myself scrolling the feed to guess what my friends have been up to, like on Venmo."
PARTICIPANT · ROUND 2

AN OPEN
MECHANIC

The prototype never resolved room lifetime: if rooms die at two minutes, what does "join later" mean, and how is a three-hour-old feed entry "ongoing"?

RETROSPECTIVE Room-lifetime exploration, worked out after the project. The 2020 prototype contains none of this:

OptionHow it worksTrade-offVerdict
Hard stopRoom dies at 2:00, period; feed entries become memories and "join" disappearsPurest promise: a call that literally cannot run longRight default, but makes the feed a graveyard
Extend by consentAt 1:45 everyone sees "+2 min?"; extends only if all tap yesCap becomes soft; "quick call" trust erodes with every extensionAllow once per room, max; unanimity keeps the pressure social, not personal
Knock to re-openA saved chatter's participants can "knock": accept within the hour and a fresh 2-minute room spawnsFeed becomes a call-starter: you don't join the old room, you knock for a new oneThe missing piece: what "join later" should have meant

So: rooms hard-stop, the feed keeps metadata forever, and every entry is a knock target that spawns a new room. "Ongoing" badges live for the room's 2 minutes only.

REFLECTIONS

The pivot taught me more than the final screens did. That spring Houseparty surged and Clubhouse launched; the appetite for drop-in rooms was real. What stayed untested, then and now, is whether a hard two-minute cap changes pickup behavior.

PROPOSED The usability study this concept still needs, never run:

ElementPlan
GoalDoes the 2-minute cap change willingness to answer, and does the room-lifetime model survive first contact?
Participants5 pairs of actual long-distance friends, ages 18–29; the unit of analysis is the pair, not the individual
TasksStart a chatter with a topic; receive one mid-task and decide answer/pass; find last week's call and knock
Success criteriaReceive-and-decide under 10 seconds without prompting; participants can explain what happens at 2:00 unaided
The real questionIn the debrief: "would you have answered without the timer?"
What this can't proveActual pickup behavior over weeks; that needs a living prototype and a diary study
WHAT I TOOK AWAY

Polite feedback with no "I'd use this" sent me back to my interview notes.
