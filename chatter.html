Chatter — the first idea did not work

A one-month solo concept for short video calls with friends. The useful part of the project was realizing that my first direction ignored the strongest thing people had told me.

PROJECTPersonal concept

TOOLSFigma · Whimsical · Miro

In March 2020, social life moved onto screens almost overnight. I wanted to find a lighter way for friends to catch up without scheduling a long call or writing paragraphs of updates.

PEOPLE LIKED CALLS,
BUT NOT THE SETUP

I interviewed eight people aged 18–25 for about 30 minutes each. Calling felt more personal and took less effort than writing a long update, but scheduling and the pressure to stay on the phone made people avoid it.

Catching up is hard because context gets lost, so conversations stay superficial.
SYNTHESIS · INSIGHT 01
Calling bonds more and tires less than texting, but scheduling it is the chore.
SYNTHESIS · INSIGHT 02
ONE BUSY FRIEND,
MANY PEOPLE TO UPDATE

I combined those patterns into Christy, a busy student who wants to share everyday updates with long-distance friends without composing the same long text several times.

“How might we redesign texting and calling to fit long-distance friendships?” THE HMW BEHIND BOTH IDEAS
FIRST TRY:
A SHARED JOURNAL

My first idea was a private journal shared with selected friend groups. One update could reach everyone, and nobody had to reply. I borrowed low-effort entry patterns from Tiny Thoughts and Daylio, then added group sharing and reminders for friends someone had not contacted lately.

THE RESPONSE WAS
POLITE AND FLAT

[PLACEHOLDER — VERIFY: I walked 6 people through the first prototype; 4 had joined the original interviews.] Nobody described a real situation where they would use it. Two comments kept coming up:

"It'd be nice to have more modes of communication, like videos or pictures."
PARTICIPANT · ROUND 1
"I still have to make efforts to update it."
PARTICIPANT · ROUND 1

I had designed a nicer way to write updates even though people told me that writing the update was the work. The stronger signal was calls: more personal, less composition. I went back and designed around the part people disliked, picking up and staying on too long.

SECOND TRY:
SHORT CALLS

Glimpse used two-minute calls between strangers. I applied the time limit to existing friendships, then borrowed the drop-in room pattern from Houseparty and Cocoon.

  • Start a chatter: open a room with a topic and a 2-minute cap
  • Save a chatter: post the call's metadata (never a recording) to your feed
  • Join a chatter: hop into a friend's live room from the feed
  • Receive a chatter: answer now, or pass without guilt
THIS TIME, PEOPLE
NAMED USE CASES

[PLACEHOLDER — VERIFY: In a second walkthrough with 7 people, 5 named a specific friend or situation where they would use the app.] The repeated complaint was the fixed two-minute limit, so I let the caller choose the cap in the high-fidelity version.

"I love it!! Such a fun and quick way to catch up with friends."
PARTICIPANT · ROUND 2
"I can see myself scrolling the feed to guess what my friends have been up to, like on Venmo."

AN OPEN
MECHANIC

The prototype never resolved room lifetime: if rooms die at two minutes, what does "join later" mean, and how is a three-hour-old feed entry "ongoing"?

After the project, I worked out one possible answer. None of it appears in the 2020 prototype:

OptionHow it worksTrade-offVerdict
Hard stopRoom dies at 2:00, period; feed entries become memories and "join" disappearsPurest promise: a call that literally cannot run longRight default, but makes the feed a graveyard
Extend by consentAt 1:45 everyone sees "+2 min?"; extends only if all tap yesCap becomes soft; "quick call" trust erodes with every extensionAllow once per room, max; unanimity keeps the pressure social, not personal
Knock to re-openA saved chatter's participants can "knock": accept within the hour and a fresh 2-minute room spawnsFeed becomes a call-starter: you don't join the old room, you knock for a new oneThe missing piece: what "join later" should have meant

So: rooms hard-stop, the feed keeps metadata forever, and every entry is a knock target that spawns a new room. "Ongoing" badges live for the room's 2 minutes only.

WHAT IS STILL
UNPROVEN

The high-fidelity prototype was never tested. The main question is still open: does a short time limit make someone more likely to answer, or does it make the call feel disposable?

The study this concept still needs:

ElementPlan
GoalDoes the 2-minute cap change willingness to answer, and does the room-lifetime model survive first contact?
Participants5 pairs of actual long-distance friends, ages 18–29; the unit of analysis is the pair, not the individual
TasksStart a chatter with a topic; receive one mid-task and decide answer/pass; find last week's call and knock
Success criteriaReceive-and-decide under 10 seconds without prompting; participants can explain what happens at 2:00 unaided
The real questionIn the debrief: "would you have answered without the timer?"
What this can't proveActual pickup behavior over weeks; that needs a living prototype and a diary study
WHERE IT LANDED

The first concept solved the wrong half of the problem. Going back to the interview notes gave me a better direction than polishing it would have.
