Morsel — see the dish before you order

Photos of dishes near you, the menu of the restaurant you’re sitting in with a photo beside each dish, and a small compare to help you decide.

ROLEProduct design, interaction design, prototyping
SCOPEIndependent product concept · 0→1 mobile app
STATUSInteractive prototype
TIMEFRAMEDecember 2025–present
TOOLSHTML/CSS/JavaScript · Claude Design and Fable
The frustrationReading a menu at the table, I want to see the dishes. The photos exist somewhere, just never next to the menu.
The designBrowse photos of dishes nearby, or say you’re at a restaurant and read its menu with photos and where each came from, then compare two or three dishes before you order.

About this case: the direction came from my own experience, and the decisions below from design exploration and prototype review. None of it has been tested with participants yet.

WHERE THIS
STARTED

Sitting at a restaurant reading a menu, I want to see the dishes. A name and a line of description only go so far, so I end up searching the restaurant and scrolling other people’s photos, hoping one of them is the dish I’m reading about.

Morsel began in December 2025 as a way to browse photos of dishes near you and then check price and distance. It only partly answered the frustration, because the moment I want a photo is at the table, with the menu in hand. During our current review I redirected the concept around that moment. The hypothesis is that if the restaurant’s menu is shown in its own order with a photo beside each dish where one exists, people can choose with fewer surprises. That is a design hypothesis, not a finding.

HOW IT
WORKS

I designed and built Morsel independently: the concept, the flows, the interface, the sample restaurants and the code. Two moments meet in it. Deciding where to go is served by photos of dishes nearby; deciding what to order is served by the menu in front of you. The feed exists to lead into a menu, and the menu is where the decision happens.

  • Nearby. Home shows restaurant cards and photos of dishes near you, nearest first. Every card leads into a restaurant’s menu.
  • I’m at a restaurant. One tap from Home opens the picker: restaurant cards with a photo strip and a coverage count, filtered as you type. No location request.
  • Read the menu. Sections in the restaurant’s order. Every dish shows its name, description and price, with a photo or a “No photo yet” block beside it. Elder & Ash is modeled in full, twelve dishes with photos for eight; nine other places carry only their photographed dishes and say so.
  • Look closer. The dish page shows each photo with its source, then the menu’s words and the listed ingredients. A photo helps you picture the food; it doesn’t confirm ingredients or today’s presentation.
  • Compare and decide. Shortlist up to three dishes and see them side by side. The Compare tab keeps count. It is a way to decide, not a cart.

THE MENU’S ORDER,
GAPS INCLUDED

The menu keeps the restaurant’s sections and order. A dish without a photo keeps its place, with a block the same size as a photo that says “No photo yet”, its description and its price. It can be opened and compared like any other. A coverage line at the top says how much of the menu has photos.

I made the menu the core because that is where the frustration happens, and kept a trimmed feed as Home because browsing photos is how you find a place; the tradeoff is that Morsel is only useful at a restaurant it covers, so every card and the picker say how much of each menu is there.

Sorting photographed dishes first would have made a prettier screen and a misleading menu. Instead there is a secondary Photos view, a grid of only the photographed dishes, which says how many it leaves out and links back to the full menu.

SAY WHERE EACH
PHOTO CAME FROM

A restaurant’s own photo and a diner’s photo of the same dish tell you different things, so every photo carries its source: “Restaurant photo” or “Diner photo”. A dish with several photos shows them one at a time with a counter, and a dish with none says so instead of borrowing a generic image.

The labels add words to every photo, and a diner’s plate may look nothing like the kitchen’s shot; I kept both because the diner’s plate is what you will be served. In this prototype both kinds are sample fixtures: nobody has contributed anything, and the labels show the provenance the product would carry.

Below the photos, the dish page keeps a photo in its place: it helps you picture the food, but it doesn’t confirm ingredients or how the dish is presented today, so the Photos line says appearance can vary between visits. The ingredients line repeats what the menu lists, names the four categories it covers, and says plainly when the menu lists nothing.

COMPARE,
NOT A CART

At a table the decision is now, between a few dishes. Any row or dish page can add a dish to a shortlist of up to three, and the Compare tab keeps count. The compare screen puts them side by side, one column each, with price, description, listed ingredients and whatever photo exists, with Remove on each and one button to add another from the menu. Ordering happens with your server.

Three is the most that fits a phone width without the descriptions becoming unreadable; the tradeoff is that someone weighing four options has to drop one first. I chose a shortlist over a cart because a cart would push toward adding, and Morsel does not take orders.

KEYBOARD AND
READABILITY CHECKS

Every card, row, toggle, tab and photo control is a real button with a visible focus ring, and the compare toggle is separate from the row so opening and shortlisting stay distinct actions. Back returns focus to the row that opened the dish, at the same scroll position. A row’s accessible name includes “No photo yet” where a sighted person sees the block. The enlarged-text example checks the layout; a full screen-reader, contrast and touch review is still to do.

WHAT THE
PROTOTYPE SHOWS

The prototype connects the whole flow: browsing nearby photos, picking a restaurant, reading a full or partial menu, opening any dish with or without photos, moving through a dish’s photos with their sources, and shortlisting and comparing up to three, with the shortlist and your place kept across screens and reloads. It shows how the design behaves when a photo is missing, when a photo came from a diner, when a menu is only partly in Morsel, and when the menu lists no ingredients. Whether any of this helps someone decide at a table is a question for the planned testing, not something the prototype can answer on its own.

WHAT I WANT
TO LEARN NEXT

I would start by asking five or six people about the last time they sat with a menu and weren’t sure what to order: whether they looked for photos, where, and what a photo settled for them. Then I would put the prototype on a table beside a printed menu and watch people pick a main within a budget, decide between two, and tell me what a dish with no photo is and where a photo came from. A separate short round would look at trust: which source people want first, and whether they read a photo as proof of portion or ingredients. The tasks and measures are in the validation plan.

LOOKING BACK

I started by building the browsing I enjoyed, and it took a review to admit the frustration was somewhere else: at the table, with a menu. The design now puts a photo, or an honest gap, beside every dish on that menu. The next step is to find out whether it helps anyone order.
