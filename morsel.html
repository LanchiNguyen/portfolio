Morsel — find a dish, then check it

A photo-first food app for one decision: pick a specific dish nearby, then see the price, the distance, and what is and isn’t known about it before you go.

ROLEProduct design, interaction design, prototyping
SCOPE0→1 mobile product concept
STATUSWorking prototype · v3.2 · not shipped
TEAM · YEARIndependent · 2026
BUILDHTML/CSS/JavaScript prototype · Claude Design and Fable
The ideaKeep browsing visual, but make price, distance, menu status and ingredient status as easy to read as the photo.
The modelTaste re-ranks. Lifestyle filters. An allergy removes dishes whose menu lists that ingredient; dishes with no ingredient information are set apart and never counted as a match.
StatusWorking prototype · v3.2 · not shipped Not evaluated with users. Restaurants, prices, menu links and “last checked” dates are illustrative fixtures.

Prototype status: the flows work; the catalog is fictional. v3.2 is a design-review revision. I audited the v3.1 build against the journey it claimed to support and rebuilt what did not hold. No usability sessions have been run; the changes below are reasoned, not yet observed.

FOOD WAS EASY TO NOTICE
AND HARD TO COMPARE

The v3.1 feed was a wall of good photos. To compare two dishes you opened each one. Search forgot the budget and distance set on the feed. The dish page led with a repeat-order score no pipeline could produce, and the order sheet asked for a checkbox that changed nothing about the dish.

So I reviewed the build against one journey and nothing else: choose an area, browse or search, narrow by budget and distance, open a dish, save it or find where to get it, and come back without losing your place. The people this is for already do this with a maps app, a delivery app and a screenshot folder. Morsel only earns a place if it removes repeated checks between an appealing photo and a choice they can act on. That advantage is a hypothesis, not a finding.

PUT THE DECISION
ON THE CARD

Every grid now uses one card: photo, dish name, restaurant and neighborhood, menu price and distance. The photo still leads; the name and the price no longer need a tap. The same card serves the feed, search, saves, collections and restaurant pages, so what you learn on one surface holds on the next.

The dish page moved its facts above the action. Price with its basis and date, distance with what it measures, menu status, and what the menu lists. Scores, diner counts and quotes are gone because the prototype has no source for them. The description is labeled as an edited menu description, not a review. Filters say “Under $15” and “1 mi” instead of dollar signs and walk times the data cannot back.

KEEP THE DINER’S
PLACE

In v3.1 the search screen owned its own query and knew nothing about the feed’s filters, so a budget set on the feed vanished in search and the query vanished on Back. In v3.2 area, filters and query live in one place. Search inherits them and shows them as chips. Back from a dish returns to the same results, the same scroll position and the card that was opened, with focus on it.

An empty result names the rule that emptied it. Nothing in the catalog, removed by a browsing filter, or removed by a dietary setting are three different answers with three different recoveries. Clearing the query keeps the filters. Clearing the filters never touches dietary settings. Nothing is widened silently, and an unsupported area never shows Washington dishes under another city’s name.

SAY ONLY WHAT
THE DATA CAN SAY

Retrospective design review. The v3.1 gate and the repeat-order score were my own earlier decisions. Removing them is a reversal from review, not from participant evidence.

v3.1 locked ordering behind “I understand this dish contains nuts.” The checkbox changed nothing about the dish; it moved responsibility. It also sat beside a score of diners who “ordered again within 90 days,” a measure that would need order attribution, consent and a follow-up window none of which exist. v3.2 removes both.

Three states, one classifier. Every surface asks the same function what a dish is relative to the diner’s settings: a match, a listed conflict, or unknown. What each surface does with the answer is policy. Feed, search and related strips recommend matches only, list unknowns apart and never show conflicts. Saves and restaurant menus show everything, labeled. A dish with no ingredient information is never given a reassuring badge, never counted as a match, and says so in plain words.

“Order” became a next step named for where it goes. A dish page, a restaurant menu, or a phone number, chosen from what is known about each restaurant, with the date the link was last checked. The demo ends with “no order was placed.” A restaurant’s own button opens its menu; it never picks a dish for you.

SAVES ARE MEMORY,
NOT RECOMMENDATION

A save is a promise to keep something, so a settings change never deletes it. Each saved card carries its state: a listed conflict, no ingredient information, or off the menu since the last check. “Recent” now sorts by a real timestamp; saves made before timestamps existed say so and keep their order rather than pretending to be new. Existing saves and collections survive the schema change.

ACCESSIBILITY —
AS IMPLEMENTED

Cards are single buttons whose name reads the dish, restaurant, price, distance and any state; the save control is a sibling, never nested. The next-step sheet is a named dialog that traps Tab, closes on Escape and returns focus to its opener. Back returns focus to the card that was opened. State chips carry a glyph as well as a color. The enlarged-text state below is a design check, not an evaluation; screen-reader, contrast and touch testing across the whole flow remain to be done.

WHAT WAS CHECKED,
AND WHAT WASN’T

Design review, September 2026. Source inspection of the v3.1 build plus a walkthrough of each screen, recorded as observation, interpretation and change. Not participant research.

  • Search ignored the feed’s filters and forgot its query on Back. Lifted area, filters and query into shared state; Back restores results, scroll and focus.
  • A missing allergen field read as “no conflict.” Added an explicit unknown state, two unknown fixtures, and one classifier every surface calls.
  • Related-dish strips bypassed dietary settings. They now draw from the same recommendable pool as the feed.
  • The restaurant’s Order button silently chose its first dish. It now opens the restaurant’s menu.
  • “Recent” sorted by catalog order. Saves carry a timestamp; older saves are labeled, not backdated.
  • “Open now,” walk times, ETAs, fees and a repeat-order score had no model behind them. Removed, with the copy that depended on them.
  • Choosing “use my location” stored a location with no city and could crash the feed. The demo area is now an explicit state; nothing is requested from the device.

Not tested: whether people compare faster with labeled cards, whether the unknown state is read as “check” rather than “fine,” whether the next-step sheet is understood as a handoff, and whether anyone would reach for this instead of a maps app. Those are the questions the plan below is built to answer, and the case will claim nothing about them until it has.

WHAT I WOULD
TEST FIRST

No sessions have been run. The first round is not about the redesign at all: five or six conversations about a recent meal decision, to learn whether the repeated-check problem is real before asking anyone to react to screens. Only then a usability round with neutral tasks that never name the intended button:

  • “You want dinner soon near the demo area. Pick something you would consider, keeping the menu price within $20.”
  • “You’re in the mood for noodles. Find an option, look at another, then go back.”
  • “You can’t decide yet. Keep two options somewhere you can find later.”
  • “Now you’re ready to get one of them. Show me what you would do next.”

A separate, smaller round with people who manage food allergies would test only comprehension of the three states: what the information says, what it leaves unanswered, and what they would check. If anyone reads “no ingredient information” as reassurance, that gets fixed before anything else.

WHERE IT LANDED

Morsel shows a dish, its price and distance, and what is and isn’t known about it, then hands off to where the dish really lives. What it cannot know, it says.
