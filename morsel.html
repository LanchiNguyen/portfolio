Morsel — find a dish, then check it

A food-discovery app for browsing dishes, comparing prices and finding somewhere to eat.

THE PROBLEM
I STARTED FROM

Choosing a meal from a food app often means opening dish after dish to learn what a photo leaves out: the price, how far away it is, and whether it fits what you can eat. Switching from browsing to search can then drop the budget and distance you had already set.

Morsel starts from a design premise, not a research finding: a photo-led feed can keep its appeal while putting those practical checks within reach. The task I designed around is simple: find a dish, compare it with another, and decide where to eat. Whether that is useful enough to replace someone’s usual search is an open question I plan to test.

WHAT I MADE
AND HOW IT WORKS

I worked on Morsel independently, from the initial concept in December 2025 to the current interactive prototype: the product idea, the flows, the interface, the sample catalog and the code. The prototype covers the whole flow rather than a single screen, which let me check how each decision holds up across the app.

  • Browse. The feed opens near a chosen area and lists dishes as labeled cards, nearest first, with price and distance filters.
  • Search. Search keeps the same area and filters and shows the same cards.
  • Check. The dish page puts price, distance, menu status and known ingredients above one primary action.
  • Save or go. Save a dish for later, or continue to the restaurant’s menu to check current prices and availability.

COMPARE DISHES
WITHOUT OPENING EACH ONE

Each card carries the dish name, restaurant, neighborhood, menu price and distance under its photo. The same card is used in the feed, in search results and in saves, so two dishes can be compared wherever you find them.

I chose the labeled card because comparing was the task; the tradeoff is a denser, less immersive feed with fewer photos per screen.

The dish page follows the same order: price, distance, menu status and listed ingredients sit above the menu button. Price filters use dollar amounts and distance is a straight-line figure in miles, because those are the facts the catalog can support. Walk times, opening hours and popularity scores are not shown, since nothing in the data backs them.

SEARCH KEEPS
YOUR FILTERS

Search shares the area, price range and distance set on the feed and shows them as chips above the results. Going back from a dish restores the query and scroll position, with keyboard focus on the card you opened.

Keeping the filters means a search can come back empty when a matching dish exists, so the empty state has to say which setting removed it; I accepted that in exchange for results that always agree with the feed.

When nothing matches, the app says why: no such dish in the catalog, excluded by price or distance, or excluded by a dietary setting. Each case has its own recovery action, and clearing browsing filters never clears dietary settings.

A MENU STEP,
NOT AN ORDER BUTTON

Morsel doesn’t take orders, so the dish page ends at the restaurant’s menu rather than a checkout. The button reads “View restaurant menu”, “View dish on menu” or “Check with restaurant”, depending on what the restaurant offers, and the sheet that follows asks you to check current prices and availability before it continues. The tradeoff is that Morsel’s usefulness stops at that handoff; in return it never shows a price, wait time or availability it cannot stand behind.

The same rule shapes the dietary states. A dish whose menu lists an ingredient you avoid is kept out of recommendations, but stays visible in saves and on restaurant pages with a warning. A dish with no ingredient information is shown as unknown, apart from the matches, and is never counted as a fit. In both cases the dish page points to other dishes and to the restaurant, rather than offering a checkbox to acknowledge the warning and carry on.

KEEP WHAT
YOU SAVED

Changing a dietary setting doesn’t make a saved dish disappear. Saved cards show ingredient conflicts, missing information and dishes that have left the menu, and “Recent” sorts by the time a dish was saved. The restaurant page lists its dishes with the same labels and opens the restaurant’s menu directly.

KEYBOARD AND
READABILITY CHECKS

Dish cards have descriptive names and Save is a separate button. The menu sheet supports Tab and Escape and returns focus to its opener when closed. Status labels use text and symbols as well as color. The enlarged-text example below checks the layout; a full screen-reader, contrast and touch review is still to do.

WHAT THE
PROTOTYPE SHOWS

  • The full flow. Entry, feed, filters, search, dish page, saves, collections, restaurant page and the menu step, with state kept between screens and across reloads.
  • One set of dietary rules. The same classification runs in the feed, search, related dishes, saves and restaurant pages, with a policy for each surface.
  • Failure and edge states. Feed loading, offline, images failing, a menu link that fails to open, an area outside coverage and a search with no match, each reachable from the controls beside the phone.
  • Sample data only. Restaurants, menus, prices and ingredient lists are fictional. There are no accounts, no live menus and no ordering.

It shows that these interactions work together. It doesn’t show whether people choose food more easily, understand the ingredient notices, or would use Morsel instead of their usual apps. That is what testing is for.

WHAT I WANT
TO LEARN NEXT

I would start by asking five or six people how they chose a recent meal, including what they checked and which apps they used. Then I would try the prototype with tasks like these:

  • “You want dinner soon, near where you are. Pick something you would consider, keeping the menu price within $20.”
  • “You’re in the mood for noodles. Find an option, look at another, then go back.”
  • “You can’t decide yet. Keep two options somewhere you can find later.”
  • “Now you’re ready to get one of them. Show me what you would do next.”

I would also ask people who manage food allergies what they understand from the ingredient notices and what they would still check with a restaurant. Missing information must not read as confirmation that a dish is suitable.

LOOKING BACK

The photos are what drew me to the idea. Working through the whole flow taught me that the details beside them are what make a dish possible to choose. The design now makes comparing easier; the next step is to find out whether it helps someone decide.
