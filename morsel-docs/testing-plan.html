Research plan

Three rounds, in order. Round A asks whether the problem is real before anyone reacts to screens. Round B tests the current flow with neutral tasks on the wireflow. Round C is a separate, smaller comprehension round on the three dietary states, with people who manage food allergies. None of the rounds has been run; every findings table is intentionally empty. Participant counts are planning ranges for qualitative work, not statistical claims.

Round A — recent behavior and problem relevance

5–6 conversations, 30–40 minutes each

  • Recruit by behavior, not by an invented persona: people who recently chose an unfamiliar casual meal in a city they know, including some who started from a photo or a saved post and some who went straight to a maps or delivery app. Include one person who tried an alternative food app and stopped.
  • Friends can pilot the script; they should not be the only evidence.
  • No screens are shown. Ask about the last relevant decision, using a non-sensitive saved post or search if they are comfortable. Do not collect unrelated messages, precise home locations, or medical details. Consent for recording; notes are enough if they prefer.
  • Do not introduce allergies as the assumed problem. Ask broadly about constraints and follow what they mention.

Prompts

  1. "Tell me about the last time you wanted something to eat but didn't know where to get it."
  2. "What happened first? What did you open next?"
  3. "What were you trying to check at each step?"
  4. "Was there a dish or place you considered and then ruled out? Why?"
  5. "How did you know you had enough information to decide?"
  6. "Did you save anything? Have you gone back to it?"
  7. "When has your usual method worked well?"
  8. "What would make trying another app not worth the effort?"
Gate. Continue with "choose a dish soon" if several independent examples show a similar unresolved step between an appealing dish and a usable choice. Reframe toward save-and-retrieve if remembering is the stronger problem. Pause the direction if the main problem is fees, group coordination, reservations, or menu accuracy this scope cannot address. A preference for attractive screens is not demand evidence.

Round B — information architecture and usability

5–6 moderated sessions, 35–45 minutes each

  • Likely users from Round A's profile, fresh where possible. Their own phone if they have one.
  • Open with a neutral statement: the catalog is simulated, the restaurants are fictional, and nothing can be ordered. Then let them choose their path; think aloud; never name the intended button.
  • Record for each task: completed unaided / completed with help / not completed; the actual obstacle; the information used. Task time is diagnostic only.

Gate. Any belief that an order was placed, that a device location was detected, or that missing ingredient information means the dish is fine is a critical comprehension issue. Fix repeated decision barriers before polishing secondary screens.

Round C — dietary information comprehension

3–4 sessions with people who manage a food allergy, plus a domain reviewer if one is available

  • Test how the information is understood. Do not ask anyone to order or eat based on the prototype.
  • Fixtures: a listed conflict, a dish with no ingredient information, a saved conflict, a related-dish route, and a restaurant page showing flagged dishes for context.
  • Collect the initial interpretation before explaining anything; then clarify the prototype's limits.
  • If no domain reviewer is available, keep the limitation and do not present the feature as validated.

The wording under test: Morsel excludes dishes whose menu lists an avoided ingredient, sets aside dishes with no ingredient information, and never calls anything safe.

Severity scale

  • S1 — trust or comprehension breach: the diner believes an order was placed, a location was detected, or missing information means the dish is fine. Fix before anything else.
  • S2 — task blocker: cannot complete a core task (compare, save, hand off) without moderator help.
  • S3 — friction: completes with detours, misclicks, or visible hesitation.
  • S4 — polish: cosmetic or preference-level.

Before / after template

For each S1–S2 finding that changes the design, produce one pair for the case study:

  1. Before: the current state and the one-line finding, with the count.
  2. After: the revised state, what changed, and why it addresses the evidence.
  3. Verdict line: the re-test criterion.

Identical crop and zoom for both frames so the change reads instantly. Retrospective design-review pairs (earlier exploration → refined design) stay labeled as review, not as findings.

No sessions have been run. The case currently documents a design review. This research would help assess whether the changes are useful to people choosing a meal.
